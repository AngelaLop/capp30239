Policy Implications

The data reveal that location matters profoundly for human capital accumulation, even within countries at similar income levels. Addressing these regional disparities requires place-based strategies that strengthen the foundations of both health and education systems. Understanding where gaps are widest—whether in child survival, life expectancy, school enrollment, or tertiary attainment—allows governments to prioritize investments with the highest social and economic returns.

Target resources toward lagging regions.

National averages often mask large internal disparities. Allocating public investment based on regional human capital diagnostics can ensure that the most disadvantaged territories—often rural, peripheral, or historically marginalized—receive focused support in health, education, and infrastructure.

Strengthen local education systems.

Policies should combine early-childhood programs, school quality improvements, and expansion of tertiary education opportunities in underserved areas. Partnerships with local universities, technical institutes, and the private sector can help align curricula with regional labor-market needs.

Enhance healthcare accessibility and quality.

In regions where life expectancy and child survival lag, investing in primary healthcare networks, mobile health services, and preventive care can reduce avoidable mortality and build a healthier, more productive workforce.

Integrate human capital into regional development planning.

Economic policies and infrastructure programs should systematically incorporate human capital metrics—for example, conditioning regional development funds on measurable progress in education and health outcomes.

Monitor and evaluate progress subnationally.

Developing standardized regional indicators and open data systems allows policymakers to track inequalities over time, identify successful interventions, and adjust strategies dynamically.

Ultimately, the relationship between human capital and economic development underscores that these investments are not only a matter of equity but of long-term growth and resilience. Regions that fail to cultivate their population's potential face rising opportunity costs, while those that close these gaps can unlock sustained and inclusive prosperity.

What is Human Capital?

Human Capital consists of the knowledge, skills and health that people invest in and accumulate throughout their lives, enabling them to realize their potential as productive members of society.

The Human Capital Index, developed by the World Bank, aims at capturing the amount of human capital a child born today could expect to attain by age 18, given the risks to poor health and education that prevail in the region she lives.

To visualize the HCI by territory, the index was built using:

Survival Rate

Will children born today survive to school age?

School Attainment

How much school will they complete?

School Enrollment

How much will they learn?

Life Expectancy

Will they leave school in good health ready for further learning and work?

Data sources

Data compiled from the OECD data explorer for each indicator. The data explorer provides regional indicators at the TL2 level for roughly 450 subnational regions across up to 48 countries, mainly OECD members. The HCI was calculated based on the World Bank Human Capital Index reports.

Why it Matters?

Human capital is a critical driver of economic development and individual well-being. By improving their skills, health, knowledge, and resilience, people can be more productive, flexible, and innovative. Human capital, and each of its components (markers of health and education), are highly correlated with productivity and wellbeing markers.

The visualizations above illustrate these relationships, showing how educational attainment, school attendance, child survival rates, and life expectancy all correlate with GDP and economic development.

Human Capital Levels and National Inequalities

Today the world is healthier and more educated than ever, and most countries have increased their human capital levels.

However, disparities remain across countries and regions. The visualization illustrates these inequalities, showing that while nearly every country has improved its Human Capital Index (HCI) since 2010, the scale and speed of progress vary significantly.

Countries in Northern and Western Europe, along with East Asia and North America, lead the global rankings with HCI values above 0.85, reflecting strong education systems and high health outcomes. In contrast, Latin American, Eastern European, and Middle Eastern countries cluster between 0.70 and 0.78, showing moderate but uneven advancement. The lowest levels are observed in parts of Latin America, with countries such as Mexico,Colombia and Brazil still below 0.71, despite meaningful gains since 2010.

Subnational Inequalities - Health

Human capital outcomes also vary widely within countries, reflecting deep subnational inequalities. When looking at the health components of the Human Capital Index, the figures illustrate that each one varies significantly across regions within each country.

In Health outcomes as Life Expectancy, these disparities are especially pronounced in large and diverse countries such as India, Russia, Mexico, Colombia, and the United States, where subnational gaps exceed 10 to 20 years in life expectancy. For example, in Colombia, the regional gap in life expectancy between the highest and lowest region (Vaupes) is 15 years, while in Japan all regions have a life expectancy above 84 years.

Similarly, in Health outcomes as Child Survival Rates, some poor regions in Colombia (Vaupes), Peru (Puno), Indonesia (Papua) and India (Madhya Pradesh) present still significantly lower levels than the national average and the rest of the countries.

Subnational Inequalities - Education

When looking at the education components of the Human Capital Index— tertiary attainment among adults (25–65 years old) and school enrollment among youth (15–19 years old)—the figures reveal large subnational gaps across nearly all countries.

In many advanced economies such as Canada, Korea, and the United Kingdom, tertiary education levels surpass 50% in the best-performing regions, while lagging regions remain 20–30 percentage points behind. Conversely, in Latin American countries like Costa Rica, Mexico, and Brazil, the share of adults with higher education remains below 40% even in the top regions, with some territories—such as Maranhão in Brazil or Oaxaca in Mexico—still below 15%.

Similarly, regional differences in secondary school enrollment highlight how opportunities for learning diverge across territories. While in countries like Poland, Portugal, and the Netherlands nearly all youth aged 15–19 remain in school, participation drops sharply in parts of Latin America and South Asia, where some subnational areas fall below 70%.

Evolution of Regional Gaps

Over time, regional inequalities in human capital have persisted—and in many cases, widened. The figure above tracks the evolution of subnational gaps in the Human Capital Index (HCI) between 2010 and 2023 across selected countries. While the overall level of human capital has risen globally, territorial disparities within countries have proved remarkably resilient.

In several Central and Eastern European countries such as Hungary, Romania, and Bulgaria, gaps have gradually increased, reflecting uneven progress across regions. Similar patterns are visible in Mexico and Colombia, where internal disparities have remained among the highest, with differences exceeding 10–15 percentage points between the most and least developed regions. By contrast, countries like Sweden, the Netherlands, and Italy exhibit relatively stable and narrow gaps, suggesting more balanced regional development.
